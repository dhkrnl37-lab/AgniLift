Engineering Energy for Unmanned Flight

AgniLift is researching how compact engines and hybrid power systems can extend the practical operating envelope of small and medium UAVs — while keeping the precise control, safety and reliability that field operations demand.

The core challenge

The energy-density question

The endurance of any aircraft depends heavily on how much usable energy it carries for each kilogram of weight. Batteries are clean, quiet and precise, and they suit a large share of UAV missions well. For operations that demand long flight times or heavier payloads, however, the weight of stored electrical energy can become a limiting factor.

Liquid fuels carry considerably more energy per kilogram than current batteries, even after accounting for the losses of a small engine. AgniLift is studying how to convert that advantage into dependable flight — an engineering problem of efficiency, control, vibration, heat and safety, not simply of adding an engine to an airframe.

Architectures

Two energy architectures under study

We are investigating where each architecture is most appropriate. Neither is treated as universally superior — the right choice depends on the mission.

PRIMARY RESEARCH DIRECTION

Series hybrid

A compact engine drives a generator, which supplies electrical energy to the propulsion system and a buffer battery. Electric motors continue to fly the aircraft, preserving the fine control that multirotor and VTOL operations rely on, while the engine provides a more energy-dense source for longer missions.

Research focus

Efficient operating points, power blending between engine and buffer battery, and smooth response to gusts and rapid manoeuvres.

FIXED-WING ENDURANCE

Direct drive

A compact engine drives the aircraft's propulsion directly, an approach well established on fuel-powered fixed-wing aircraft. It is mechanically simpler and efficient in cruise, making it a candidate for long-range observation, surveying and mapping where vertical hover is not required.

Research focus

Engine–propeller matching, cruise efficiency, and reliable long-duration operation.

See the three-path propulsion overview → A parallel-hybrid configuration, where engine and motor contribute together, is also of interest for peak-power and heavy-payload research.

Integration Challenges

Turning an engine into a dependable aircraft

An engine is only useful in the air if the surrounding system manages its side effects well. These integration problems are central to our research.

Vibration management

Engines introduce vibration that can affect sensors, cameras and structures. We are studying isolation and balancing so payloads and avionics operate cleanly.

Thermal management

Combustion produces heat that must be directed and dissipated. Cooling-airflow design and component placement are important research areas.

Engine monitoring

Continuous-duty flight benefits from health monitoring — sensing engine and generator condition to support safety and maintenance decisions.

Flight-control integration

The propulsion system must work with established flight-control approaches so operators keep precise, predictable handling.

Fuel-system safety

Safe fuel storage, delivery and handling are treated as first-order design requirements from the outset.

Modular payload interfaces

Standardised mechanical, power and data interfaces so a platform can carry different mission payloads with minimal rework.

Open Questions

Research questions we are working on

  • How can compact engines be operated efficiently and reliably under continuous UAV flight loads?
  • How should energy be managed between engine, generator and buffer battery across a mission?
  • What vibration and thermal strategies best protect sensitive payloads?
  • How can fuel handling and engine operation be made safe and simple for field crews?
  • Which missions genuinely benefit from hybrid power, and which are better served by batteries?
